# Bonding Curves

Every Superhero community token is priced by one deterministic function of its own supply. Buying
mints new tokens and moves you up the curve; selling burns tokens and moves you down it. Because
price is computed from supply, there is always a quote and always liquidity — no order book, no
matching engine, no counterparty on the other side of your trade.

Superhero uses an **exponential** curve, implemented by the `BondingCurveExponential` contract. The
contract evaluates `e^x` as a bounded Taylor series in integer arithmetic — at most 100 terms, and
stopping as soon as a term floors to zero — which is what `curve_type()` means when it returns
`"TAYLOR_EXPONENTIAL_V1"`. The series agrees with the closed form below to within `4e-19 AE` at
every supply in the examples below. The integer the contract returns is the price you are charged;
[Curve Parameters & Units](/tokens/curve-parameters) has a reference implementation that reproduces
it exactly.

## The formulas

```text
buy(x)  = 0.001 · e^(0.00000001 · x) − 0.0009999

sell(x) = buy(x) · 0.995
```

`x` is the circulating supply in whole tokens; the result is the **instantaneous (spot) price in AE
per token** at that supply. Three constants do all the work:

| Constant | Value | Stored on-chain as | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `a` | 0.001 | `a = 10^15` | Scale factor on the exponential |
| `k` | 0.00000001 (1e-8) | `k = 10^10` | Growth rate per token of supply |
| `c` | 0.0009999 | not stored | Offset that pulls the launch price down to a near-zero number |

The contract holds `a` and `k` scaled by `10^18`, plus `initial_price = 10^11` — the spot price at
supply zero, `0.0000001 AE`. The offset `c` is `a − initial_price` and is never stored. See
[Curve Parameters & Units](/tokens/curve-parameters) for the full constant table.

At supply 0 the spot price is `0.001 − 0.0009999 = 0.0000001 AE` — one ten-millionth of an AE per
token. The curve is almost flat at the start and steep later.

What you pay is never the spot price. It is the **integral** of the curve across the range of supply
you are minting into:

```text
buy price   = ∫ from supply to (supply + amount) of buy(x) dx

sell return = ∫ from (supply − amount) to supply of sell(x) dx
```

The spread — the community DAO's share — is the area between the two curves:

```text
spread = ∫ from 0 to supply of buy(x) dx  −  ∫ from 0 to supply of sell(x) dx
```

## Integrating the curve

For `F'(x) = a·e^(k·x) − c`:

```text
F(x) = (a / k) · e^(k · x) − c · x

with a = 0.001, k = 0.00000001, c = 0.0009999:

a / k = 0.001 / 0.00000001 = 100000

F(x) = 100000 · e^(0.00000001 · x) − 0.0009999 · x
```

So the cost of buying `n` tokens starting from supply `S` is:

```text
cost(S, n) = F(S + n) − F(S)
           = 100000 · ( e^(0.00000001·(S+n)) − e^(0.00000001·S) ) − 0.0009999 · n
```

That is the whole pricing model. Everything below is arithmetic on this one line.

## Worked example 1 — 1,000 tokens at supply 0

```text
cost(0, 1000) = 100000 · ( e^(0.00000001 · 1000) − e^0 ) − 0.0009999 · 1000
              = 100000 · ( e^0.00001 − 1 ) − 0.9999

e^0.00001      = 1.000010000050000166667…
e^0.00001 − 1  = 0.000010000050000166667…
× 100000       = 1.0000050000166667…
− 0.9999       = 0.0001050000166667…

cost = 0.000105000017 AE   (about 0.000105 AE)
```

The average price paid is `0.000105000017 / 1000 = 1.05000017e-7 AE` per token, against a spot
price of `1.0e-7 AE` when you started. You paid **5.00% above spot** for a thousand tokens, because
by the thousandth token the spot price had already risen to `1.10e-7 AE`.

## Worked example 2 — 1,000 tokens at supply 10,000,000

```text
cost(10000000, 1000)
  = 100000 · ( e^(0.00000001 · 10001000) − e^(0.00000001 · 10000000) ) − 0.9999
  = 100000 · ( e^0.10001 − e^0.1 ) − 0.9999

e^0.1       = 1.10517091807564762481…
e^0.10001   = 1.10518196984008711139…
difference  = 0.00001105176444865756…
× 100000    = 1.10517644394865756…
− 0.9999    = 0.10527644394865756…

cost = 0.105276443949 AE
```

The same thousand tokens now cost **0.10528 AE instead of 0.000105 AE — about 1,003 times more**.
Average price `1.052764e-4 AE`, spot price at supply 10,000,000 `1.052709e-4 AE`: this time you
paid only **0.0052% above spot**.

**The same trade size is proportionally far more expensive near supply zero than deep into the
curve.** Near zero, the `−0.0009999` offset has cancelled almost all of `0.001·e^(kx)`, so the tiny
residual price doubles over a very short span of supply. Further out, the offset is negligible and
the curve is locally almost linear, so a thousand tokens barely move it.

## Curve slippage

Slippage on a bonding curve is a property of the integral. You are charged the area under a rising
curve, but the quoted spot price is only the height at the left-hand edge of that area. The bigger
the slice, the further the right-hand edge has risen above it, and the worse your average fill.

All of these are buys starting from supply 10,000,000, where spot is `1.052709e-4 AE`:

| Tokens bought | Total AE cost | Average price (AE) | Average vs spot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 | 0.105276 | 1.05276e-4 | +0.0052% |
| 100,000 | 10.582369 | 1.05824e-4 | +0.525% |
| 1,000,000 | 110.815238 | 1.10815e-4 | +5.267% |
| 10,000,000 | 1,624.184008 | 1.62418e-4 | +54.29% |
| 50,000,000 | 21,699.788231 | 4.33996e-4 | +312.3% |

Buying 10,000,000 tokens in one transaction doubles the supply, and you pay a 54% premium to the
price you saw before you clicked. Splitting the order does **not** help: the curve is
path-independent, so ten buys of 1,000,000 from a rising supply cost exactly the same total as one
buy of 10,000,000. The only things that reduce your average price are buying less, or buying when
the supply is lower.

A sell works the same way in reverse: you receive the area under the *sell* curve as supply falls
back, so a large sell realises an average price below the spot price you were quoted.
`minimal_return_required` protects you from that — see
[Buying & Selling](/tokens/buying-and-selling).

## Shape of the curve

Both curves over the first 100 million tokens of supply, with `#` the buy curve and `.` the sell
curve where it separates:

```text
        price (AE per token)
 1.72e-03 |                                                      ##
          |                                                    ##.
          |                                                 ###
 1.37e-03 |                                               ##.
          |                                            ###
          |                                         ###
 1.03e-03 |                                      ###
          |                                   ###
          |                               ####
 6.87e-04 |                           ####
          |                       ####
          |                   ####
 3.44e-04 |              #####
          |         #####
          |    #####
 0.00e+00 |####
          +--------------------------------------------------------
          0             25M           50M           75M        100M
                              supply (tokens)
```

The sell curve sits 0.5% below the buy curve everywhere. The gap between them is the DAO's cut —
small per trade, meaningful in aggregate. Spot prices at a few reference supplies:

| Supply | Buy spot (AE) | Sell spot (AE) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1.000000e-7 | 9.950000e-8 |
| 1,000,000 | 1.015017e-5 | 1.009942e-5 |
| 10,000,000 | 1.052709e-4 | 1.047446e-4 |
| 50,000,000 | 6.488213e-4 | 6.455772e-4 |
| 100,000,000 | 1.718382e-3 | 1.709790e-3 |
| 200,000,000 | 6.389156e-3 | 6.357210e-3 |

The curve is tuned on the expectation that only **10–20% of the total supply** will ever be reached,
which is why the interesting price action is packed into the first couple of hundred million tokens.

## Why the reserve is always solvent

The sell curve sits strictly below the buy curve at every supply level, so the AE paid into the sale
contract always exceeds the AE it could ever be asked to pay out. Selling the entire circulating
supply back to the curve is always possible.

At a supply of 10,000,000 tokens, buyers have paid in `518.09 AE` in total, and selling every one of
those tokens back would return `515.50 AE`. The `2.59 AE` difference is the DAO's accumulated
spread, and it never has to be given back.

## The spread

The `sell()` curve returns 99.5% of `buy()`, and the sale contract adds a 0.5% affiliation fee on
buys, so the **total spread between buy and sell price is 1% of the curve value**: 0.5% to the
community DAO and 0.5% to the affiliation system.

From the buyer's side: you hand over `1.005 ×` the curve integral and can immediately sell back for
`0.995 ×` it, so a round trip at unchanged supply returns `0.995 / 1.005 = 0.99502…` of what you
paid — a **0.995% loss on an instant round trip**. See [Fees & Slippage](/tokens/fees-and-slippage)
for the full breakdown and [Risks](/tokens/risks) for what that means for a position.

## Compared with other curve families

| Property | Superhero (exponential) | Linear curve | Constant product (Uniswap-style) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price rule | price = a·e^(k·supply) − c | price = m·supply + b | price = reserve_y / reserve_x, with x·y = k |
| What backs it | AE reserve accumulated from buys | AE reserve accumulated from buys | Two-sided pool funded by liquidity providers |
| Who supplies liquidity | Nobody — the contract mints and burns | Nobody | Liquidity providers, who can withdraw |
| Supply | Elastic: minted on buy, burned on sell | Elastic | Fixed; the pool only swaps existing tokens |
| Price at zero activity | Defined by the formula | Defined by the formula | Undefined until someone seeds the pool |
| Cost of a trade | Integral of the curve over the minted range | Integral of the curve over the minted range | Determined by the invariant x·y = k |
| Price impact of a big buy | Compounding — grows exponentially | Proportional to size | Grows as the pool is drained; unbounded |
| Fee model | 1% spread baked into two curves | Implementation-defined | Explicit swap fee (e.g. 0.30%) to LPs |
| Rug-pull surface | No LP to withdraw | No LP to withdraw | LPs can remove liquidity |

Two practical consequences. There is no liquidity to pull: the reserve is held by the sale contract
and is sufficient to redeem everything. And an exponential curve punishes large late buys much
harder than a constant-product pool of comparable depth — the price impact of doubling the supply
is a factor of `e^(k·supply)`, not a bounded ratio.

Community tokens trade only against their own curve. Superhero's AMM for ordinary token pairs is
[the DEX](/defi/swap).

## Reading the curve from the contract

| Entrypoint | Returns |
| --- | --- |
| `calculate_buy_price(total_supply, count)` | Cost to buy `count` tokens from `total_supply`, in aettos |
| `calculate_sell_return(total_supply, sell_tokens)` | AE returned for selling `sell_tokens`, in aettos |
| `buy_curve(x)` / `sell_curve(x)` | The spot curve value as a `Frac.frac` rational |
| `buy_price(total_supply)` / `sell_price(total_supply)` | The same value rounded to an integer — see below |
| `get_k()`, `get_initial_price()`, `get_minimum_count()` | `10^10`, `10^11`, `10^12` |
| `get_sell_return_percentage()` | `9950` — the sell factor is `9950 / 10000 = 0.995` |
| `curve_type()`, `supported_decimals()` | `"TAYLOR_EXPONENTIAL_V1"`, `18` |

Every one of these takes and returns integers in the contract's own units — read
[Curve Parameters & Units](/tokens/curve-parameters) before calling them. Passing whole tokens
where the contract expects 18-decimal units gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of 10^18.

`buy_price()` and `sell_price()` round a value like `1.05e-4 AE` per token to an integer, so
`buy_price()` returns `1` and `sell_price()` returns `0` for every supply under roughly 690 million
tokens. Use `calculate_buy_price` and `calculate_sell_return` for quotes, or `buy_curve` for the
exact spot value.

`get_minimum_count()` returns `10^12`, and every pricing call requires `count >= minimum_count`
(`MINIMUM_TOKENS_REQUIRED`). Since counts are in 18-decimal units that floor is `10^-6` tokens — a
guard against a trade rounding to zero inside the integer arithmetic, not an order minimum. It fires
when a caller passes a whole-token count where the contract expected token decimal units.
